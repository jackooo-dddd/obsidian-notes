#### $C_i$: Worst-Case Execution Time (CPU Processing Time)
If $C_i=3,$ it means a job generated by $\tau_i$ may require up to 3 time units of CPU execution.
It does not mean that the job executes continuously for 3 time units as it may be preempted.
- $C_i$ (WCET): How much CPU time the job needs to finish.
- $D_i$ (Deadline): How much real-world time the job is allowed to take.
#### $T_i$: Period
For a periodic task, $T_i=10$ means jobs are released every 10 time units: $0,\ 10,\ 20,\ 30,\ldots$
In general, $r_{i,j+1}=r_{i,j}+T_i$ for periodic task, the release time of the $J_{i, j+1}$  is $T_i$ units time units later than the release time of $J_{i, j}$.
- For a **sporadic task**, $T_i$ represents the minimum inter-arrival time. For example, if $T_i=10,$ the jobs may arrive at $0,\ 12,\ 25,\ 35,\ldots$ because consecutive jobs are always separated by at least 10 time units.
The arrival of a job does not mean the job can be executed. Arrival is when a job becomes available (enters the ready queue). When a new job arrives:
1. **If the CPU is idle** → the new job may start executing immediately.
2. **If the CPU is busy** with another job → the new job waits in the ready queue.
3. **If the new job has higher priority** (in a preemptive scheduler like EDF or RMS) → it **preempts** the currently running job and starts executing right away, even though the previous job isn't finished. The preempted job goes back to the ready queue.
#### $D_i$: Relative Deadline
Suppose $D_i=8$, and a job is released at $r_{i, j}=20$. Its absolute deadline is $d_{i,j}=r_{i,j}+D_i.$
Therefore, $d_{i,j}=20+8=28.$
$\boxed{D_i=\text{relative deadline}=\text{The maximum time allowed for a job from Task i to execute}}$  $\boxed{d_{i,j}=\text{absolute deadline}=\text{At which timestamp the Job j released from Task i should be finished}}.$
#### $R_i$: Response Time
- **Response Time of a Single Job ($R_{i, j}$):** For a specific job`j`, the response time is simply the total time it spends in the system from the moment it arrives until it finishes. It includes both the time it spent waiting in the ready queue AND the time it spent executing on the CPU. $R_{i,j}=f_{i,j}-r_{i,j}.$
- **Worst-Case Response Time of a Task ($R_i$):** Jobs can arrive at different times and face different amount of interference from other tasks, every single job $j$ from Task i will have its own unique response time. The Worst-Case Response Time ($R_i$) of the task is the maximum response time among _all possible jobs_ of that task under the worst possible scheduling conditions. $R_i = max(R_{i, j})\text{ for all jobs j.}
- Why do we care about $R_i$? We compare the Worst-Case Response Time ($R_i$) against the Relative Deadline ($D_i$) to prove that the system is schedulable.
#### $U_i$: Task Utilization
The utilization of task $\tau_i$ is $U_i=\frac{C_i}{T_i}.$
For $\tau_1=(1,4),$ we have $U_1=\frac{1}{4}=0.25.$
For $\tau_2=(2,10),$ we have $U_2=\frac{2}{10}=0.2.$ 
Therefore, total utilization is $U=U_1+U_2=0.25+0.2=0.45.$
So these two tasks demand, on average, about **45% of one CPU's capacity**.
In general, $\boxed{U=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{C_i}{T_i}}$ 

#### Task ($\tau_i$) and Job ($J_{i,j}$)
**Task ($\tau_i$):** This is the abstract, recurring program (like a sensor reading loop). It lives forever and releases new work over and over.
**Job ($J_{i,j}$)**: This is a _single, concrete instance_ of that task (e.g., the 3rd time the sensor reading loop runs). $J_{i,j}$ represents the $j$-th job generated by task $i$.
Once $J_{i,j}$ is released at time $r_{i,j}$, it goes through a strict lifecycle and never "release" again. 
$f_{i,j}$ is its completion time, the time the job actually finishes (reality) 
$d_{i,j}$ is the time by which the job should or must finish (constraint) 
The response time of $J_{i,j}$ is $R_{i,j}=f_{i,j}-r_{i,j}.$ 
To meet its deadline, $f_{i,j}\le d_{i,j}.$ equivalently, $R_{i, j} \le D_i$  
**What happens when a job takes longer than expected:** The CPU does not stop executing a job because the clock hit the deadline. The job will keep running on the CPU until it actually finishes its code, _unless_ the operating system is programmed to stop it. How the system reacts to deadline miss depends on the type of real-time system you are building
 **What $J_{i,j}$ experiences :**
1. **Released / Arrives:** It enters the system and goes into the **Ready Queue**. (This happens exactly once).
2. **Executing:** The scheduler picks it, and it runs on the CPU. Once a job starts executing, it runs straight through to the finish line unless a higher-priority job pushes it off the CPU.
3. **Preempted (Optional):** If a higher-priority job arrives, this job is paused and sent **back to the Ready Queue**. _(Notice it goes back to Ready, it does NOT "re-arrive")_.
4. **Resumes:** It gets the CPU again and continues executing.
5. **Terminates / Completes:** It finishes its work at time $f_{i,j}$, and leaves the system forever.
#### Priority Sets: $hp(i)$ and $lp(i)$
$hp(i)$ denotes the set of tasks with **higher priority** than $\tau_i$.
$lp(i)$ denotes the set of tasks with **lower priority** than $\tau_i$.
For example, suppose $\tau_1 > \tau_2 > \tau_3$ 
Then: $hp(1)=\varnothing$, $hp(2)=\{\tau_1\}$, $hp(3)=\{\tau_1,\tau_2\}.$ 
Higher-priority tasks can preempt lower-priority tasks and therefore create **interference**.

#### Blocking Time $B_i$
$B_i$ represents the maximum time for which task $\tau_i$ may be **blocked** by another task.
Blocking commonly happens because of shared locks, critical sections, non-preemptive execution or shared hardware resources. 
$$
\boxed{
R_i
=
C_i
+
B_i
+
I_i
}
$$
- $C_i$: own execution
- $B_i$: blocking
- $I_i$: interference from other tasks

**Example**
Consider the real-time task
$$
\tau_1=(C_1=1,\;T_1=4,\;D_1=4)
$$
- $\tau_1$: Task 1
- $C_1=1$: each job of Task 1 requires at most **1 time unit of CPU execution**
- $T_1=4$: Task 1 releases a new job every **4 time units** if it is periodic
- $D_1=4$: each job must finish within **4 time units after its release**
Its jobs may arrive like this:
```text
time:   0   1   2   3   4   5   6   7   8 ...
        │               │               │
       J1,1            J1,2            J1,3
```
- Job $J_{1,1}$ is released at time $0$ and must finish by time $4$
- Job $J_{1,2}$ is released at time $4$ and must finish by time $8$
- Job $J_{1,3}$ is released at time $8$ and must finish by time $12$
---
#### Common Fixed-Priority Response-Time Equation
For preemptive fixed-priority scheduling without blocking, a very common response-time equation is
$$
R_i
=
C_i+
\sum_{\tau_j\in hp(i)}
\left\lceil
\frac{R_i}{T_j}
\right\rceil C_j.
$$
- $R_i$: worst-case response time of task $\tau_i$
- $C_i$: execution time of $\tau_i$
- $T_j$: period of higher-priority task $\tau_j$
- $C_j$: execution time of higher-priority task $\tau_j$

$\left\lceil\frac{R_i}{T_j}\right\rceil$ represents how many jobs of higher-priority task $\tau_j$ may interfere during $\tau_i$'s response-time window. The longer $\tau_i$'s response time is, the more jobs interfere it.
Multiplying by $C_j$ gives the total interference caused by $\tau_j$: $\left\lceil\frac{R_i}{T_j}\right\rceil C_j.$   
Therefore,
$$
\boxed{
R_i
=
\underbrace{C_i}_{\text{own execution}}
+
\underbrace{
\sum_{\tau_j\in hp(i)}
\left\lceil
\frac{R_i}{T_j}
\right\rceil C_j
}_{\text{higher-priority interference}}
}
=C_i+I_i
$$
